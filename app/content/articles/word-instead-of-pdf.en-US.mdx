## The decision, up front

A public procurement planning system generates three documents required by law. I made it export **Word**, not PDF.

If you work with software, something in you reacted to that sentence. PDF is immutable, universal, preserves formatting, does not depend on an office suite, and is what any developer would pick in five seconds. I agree with all of it.

And PDF would still have killed the system.

## Why PDF is almost always right

The argument deserves its full strength before being contradicted.

A document that has to be archived, audited or published wants to be immutable. If anyone can open and change it, the file stops being evidence of anything. In public procurement, where the document defines what the government is about to buy, that property looks essential.

That is why PDF is the standard. It is not fashion: it is the correct answer to "this artifact needs to be trustworthy in the future".

## The process the software does not control

What changes everything is what happens to the document **after** it is generated.

Inside a city government, it does not end at the download. It physically travels between departments. Along the way, a public employee needs to fix wording that turned out ambiguous, add a justification the legal team asked for, correct a figure that changed since last week, and sign the printout.

That circuit was not designed by anyone on the technology team, is not documented anywhere, and is not going to change because a new system went live.

With PDF, each of those steps becomes friction. And friction does not spread evenly: it piles up on the weakest point, which is the person who has to get the process through by Friday.

## The predictable ending

Here is the part people who have never shipped software inside an organization tend to miss: **when the system gets in the way of the process, it is not the process that changes.**

The department is not going to reform its approval circuit because the new tool prefers PDF. It will generate the PDF, open an editor next to it, retype the content, and move on. Within a month, the system is an extra step. Within three, someone asks why they are paying for it.

And the damage does not stop there. The next system the technology team proposes will meet an organization that has already learned that systems get in the way. Trust is spent once and comes back slowly.

## What the "worse" choice bought

By exporting Word, the document starts inside the system — standardized, with an audit trail, with a record of who created it and when — and keeps flowing the way the organization knows how to work.

Notice what was gained: **traceability of origin**, which is what was actually missing. The problem was never that the document was editable; it was that nobody knew it existed, who wrote it, or which demand it came from. The system solves that, and solves it without requiring anyone to change behavior.

Immutability, which PDF would bring, was a desirable property. Adoption was a necessary one. When the two collide, necessary wins.

## Generalizing it without turning it into an excuse

This reasoning is dangerous, because applied lazily it justifies anything. "Users are used to it" has defended a lot of bad interfaces.

The distinction I use is this: **the human process is a system constraint, not a user preference.**

- If the behavior exists because the old tool was bad, change the behavior. That is migration, and the friction is worth it.
- If the behavior exists because a law, an audit or a physical circuit requires it, it is a constraint. Design for it, even when it hurts technically.

A signature on paper inside a government department is not a user preference: it is the real approval process, with a legal basis, involving people who do not report to the product team.

## The takeaway

Software inside an organization is not judged by the purity of its choices. It is judged by whether it crosses the process that already exists without breaking it.

The question I now ask before settling any format, integration or approval flow is always the same: **what happens to this artifact after it leaves my system?** If I cannot answer, I do not understand the problem yet — and whatever technically elegant decision I make has a good chance of being the wrong one.
