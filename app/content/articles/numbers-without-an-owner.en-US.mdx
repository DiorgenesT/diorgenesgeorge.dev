## The sentence that became a rule

I wrote this in the repository of one of the systems I maintain, and now apply it to all of them: **wrong data for a public official is worse than no data.**

It sounds dramatic until you follow the chain. A dashboard displays an indicator. Someone decides based on it — where to allocate staff, which neighborhood to prioritize, which contract to renew. If the number was wrong, the decision was not bad for lack of information: it was bad *with confidence*, because the person deciding believed they were looking at reality.

No dashboard at all would have produced a question. A wrong dashboard produced a certainty.

## The symptom: the number nobody can source

The pattern always looks the same. You open a dashboard and see a card with a big number. You ask where it comes from.

The bad answers are familiar: "it comes from the department's spreadsheet" (which one?), "the system calculates it" (from what?), "it was like that when I got here" (the most honest one).

And there is the worst of them, the hardcoded number — put there as an example during development, approved in a demo, never replaced. It is not wrong by a calculation accident: it never had any relationship with reality. And on screen it is indistinguishable from the others.

## Two questions that settle it

Before any indicator goes into a dashboard I build, it has to answer two things:

1. **What is the origin?** Not "the department", but the artifact: which file, which table, which endpoint, which row.
2. **Who updates it, and how often?** An indicator without an owner is not updated; it is abandoned. And an abandoned indicator stays on screen looking current.

If both answers do not exist, the indicator does not go in. This is not rigor for its own sake: displaying a number without those answers transfers to the reader a confidence nobody can back.

## Origin as a field, not as documentation

In one migration, around 468 agreements came from nine incompatible spreadsheets, one per department. The temptation is to normalize everything, ship the nice dashboard, and write down in a README where the data came from.

I did it differently: **every record stores its source file as a field of the data itself.**

The difference shows up at the first conflict. Someone opens the dashboard, sees a number that does not match their memory, and the conversation starts. Without the field, you are defending the ETL — arguing that your code is right against another person's recollection, and nobody wins that argument. With the field, you defend nothing: you point. This record came from that file, that row. Let's look together.

Within three months of operation, that changes the politics of the system. The dashboard stops being "what the technology team says" and becomes "what your own data says, organized".

## When a spreadsheet is the right interface

One consequence developers dislike: sometimes the right way to solve origin is **a spreadsheet**.

On an executive dashboard, the indicators were hardcoded and needed owners. The obvious answer would be to build a CRUD — forms, validation, permissions, audit. It would take weeks and require every department to learn a new system in order to type twelve numbers a year.

Instead, I generated structured spreadsheets, one per area, with the right cells, targets and previous-year comparison by formula. The department maintains what it already knows how to maintain, and every cell has an identifiable owner.

The criterion I use: **when data changes a few times a year, has a clear owner and needs no complex validation, a spreadsheet is the right interface.** Building a CRUD for that solves the developer's problem, not the user's.

But mind the limit, which is where many people get it wrong: what is **computed** is not typed in. Projections, composite indices, correlations — those stay derived, because typing in the result of a calculation creates a second source of truth that will diverge from the first at some point, quietly.

## The takeaway

A management dashboard is a promise: what is here is true enough for you to decide on. Every unsourced number is a small hole in that promise, and holes do not stay local — once one indicator turns out to be wrong, the official starts doubting the whole screen, including the parts that were right.

That is why the correct answer to "we don't have that reliably yet" is never to fill it with an estimate. It is to leave the space empty, with a sentence saying what is missing. A dashboard with four trustworthy numbers is worth more than one with twelve, three of which are guesses.
