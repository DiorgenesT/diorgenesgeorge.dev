## The problem

The city's performance against the Sustainable Development Goals was already public — it just was not accessible. It lived in annual PDF reports and in official statistical databases that require knowing exactly which table to open, which territorial level to pick, and which variable to request.

Citizens, journalists and oversight councils had a right to the data and no path to it.

## The decision that makes the dashboard mean something

Every indicator is imported at three levels: Betim, Minas Gerais and Brazil.

It reads like a scoping detail, and it is what decides whether the dashboard communicates at all. "Infant mortality of X" tells a reader nothing — is X high? low? improving? Next to Minas Gerais and Brazil, the same number becomes immediately readable, and the next question stops being "is this good?" and becomes "why?".

Without comparison, an isolated indicator is a number on a screen. With it, it is information.

## The part nobody sees

The index that consolidates municipal performance changes structure from year to year. The most recent edition carries a broad time series, but some codes were discontinued and exist only in the oldest edition; an intermediate edition has no series at all and enters as a point measurement for that year.

I built a unified catalog with an explicit precedence rule, newest edition first, and kept that index's indicators under a separate synthetic target so they never mix with the official UN indicators.

It is the invisible part of the work, and it is what decides whether the time series lies. A chart that silently stitches three methodologies together produces a beautiful, false line.

## An architecture decision about access

This dashboard was deliberately left out of the consolidation of internal dashboards. That consolidated application sits entirely behind institutional login, and this one is public by definition: migrating it would have removed access for citizens, which is the reason it exists.

## The result

The dashboard shipped as **ODS Betim**.

Indicators that were scattered across statistical databases and annual reports became something anyone can look up, with historical series and comparison against Minas Gerais and Brazil. Every source is public, and each one has its origin table identified in the loading process.
