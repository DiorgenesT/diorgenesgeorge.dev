## The problem

To know which emergency room was busiest, a person had to go there and look at the queue. The information existed — Betim's clinical regulation system knew how many people were at each stage — but it sat behind an internal system, far from whoever had to decide where to take a child with a fever at eleven at night.

## The decision

The naive path is to query the source on every visit to the dashboard. It works in testing and fails exactly when it matters: on the day the dashboard is announced, or on the night many people fall ill at once, citizen traffic turns into load on the system health workers are using to do their jobs.

**The source is not queried per visit.** A scheduled task every fifteen minutes fetches the data and writes the result into edge key-value storage. The interface polls only that storage, every thirty seconds.

The effect is what matters: a thousand people opening the dashboard at once generate zero additional requests against the clinical system. Read cost became independent of audience, and live health data is never one click away from overload.

The dashboard also has two source paths — the regulation system's API when credentials are available, and published spreadsheets as a fallback. With neither, the screen shows an empty state instead of breaking. A system the public consults to make health decisions cannot show a blank page.

## The decision nobody sees

The second choice is less glamorous and just as decisive: **the parser accepts what the operator actually types.**

Waiting times arrive written every possible way — with colons, with the word "min", as "1h30", or as a bare number. Column matching ignores accents, casing and stray whitespace. None of it is elegant, and it is the difference between a system that works on paper and one that survives real human data entry, done by someone in the middle of a shift.

## The result

The dashboard shipped as **UPA Agora**.

Information that existed only inside the unit became public and checkable before leaving home. People who need care choose a unit by its queue rather than only by distance — and the people working in that unit never feel any of it as load.
